SDLC: From Idea to Done - Recap

Try to answer each question first, then click it to see the answer.

1. What is SDLC

1. What does SDLC stand for, and what is its goal?

Software Development Life Cycle: a structured process to plan, build, test, deliver and maintain software. The goal is the right product, built right, delivered predictably.

2. Name the seven SDLC phases in order.

Requirements, Analysis, Design, Implementation, Testing, Deployment, Maintenance.

3. What artifact does each SDLC phase produce?
  • Requirements: vision, epics, stories with acceptance criteria
  • Analysis: feasibility notes, risk list, estimates
  • Design: architecture diagram, interface and data contracts
  • Implementation: working code and a reviewed pull request
  • Testing: test cases traced to acceptance criteria, and test reports
  • Deployment: a released increment, runbook, release notes
  • Maintenance: monitoring, defect reports, change requests

If a phase produces no artifact, you cannot show it happened.

4. Which roles take part in the SDLC, and what does each one own?
  • Product Owner / Business Analyst: the problem, the priorities and acceptance
  • Architect: the technical direction and the non-functional requirements
  • Developer: the plan, the code and the tests
  • Reviewer: that the change is correct, safe and maintainable
  • QA: that the tests actually prove the acceptance criteria
  • Release / Ops: that it ships and keeps running

Distinguish the user you build for, who drives the value, from the approvers, who are the reason the gates exist.

5. When AI is used across the SDLC, what is the governing rule?

AI prepares the evidence; people make the decisions. Every gate stays a named human approval, and nothing reaches a customer or an external party until a person has approved it.

AI accelerates each step. It never owns the decision, and it never holds the accountability.

2. Waterfall vs Agile

6. How does Waterfall work, and when does it fit?

Phases run in sequence, and each one is signed off before the next starts. Change after sign-off is a change request. It fits stable, fixed, often regulated scope.

7. Under Waterfall and under Agile, when does the first real user feedback arrive?

Waterfall: not until user acceptance testing, near the end - on a 30-week project, around week 28. Any misunderstanding in the requirements phase stays invisible until then, which is why it is so expensive to fix.

Agile: at the first sprint review, around week 4, and every sprint after that. Same total work, but the feedback loop is weeks instead of months.

8. Name the four Agile Manifesto values.
  • Individuals and interactions over processes and tools
  • Working software over comprehensive documentation
  • Customer collaboration over contract negotiation
  • Responding to change over following a plan

"Over" does not mean "instead of".

9. What is the V-Model, and where is it still common?

Waterfall with a test phase mirrored to each design phase. It is still common in regulated and automotive contexts.

10. Under Agile, when and where does a stakeholder first see working software?

At the sprint review, every two weeks - working software, not a status report or a slide. Under Waterfall the same stakeholder might wait seven months.

That is the whole point of the cadence: the stakeholder can correct course while correcting is still cheap.

3. Agile inception and the end-to-end flow

11. Put the Agile flow in order, from Inception to Done.

Inception, Epic Map, High-level Estimation, Release Plan (Milestones), Epics, Stories, Sprint Planning, Plan, Code, Review, Test, Done.

12. What is an agile inception, how long should it take, and what is the real output?

A collaborative project-launch workshop at the start of delivery, run in 1 to 5 days (a Lean Inception fits one week). It unites stakeholders, product owners and the delivery team to agree a shared vision, scope and plan.

Days, not months. And the real output is not a document set - it is a room full of people who agree on the same MVP. A team that produces every artifact but changes no outcome has failed.

13. Name the ten activities of the Agile Inception Deck.
  • Why are we here? (vision / business case)
  • The elevator pitch
  • The product box (or cover story)
  • The NOT list (out of scope)
  • User personas and roles
  • Feature discovery and user story mapping
  • Technical solutions and architecture
  • What keeps us up at night? (risk assessment)
  • Trade-off sliders (scope vs time vs budget vs quality)
  • Sizing and the first release (MVP definition)
14. For three inception activities, name the data collected and the expected outcome.
  • Why are we here? Data: business drivers, problem statement, KPIs, strategic objectives. Outcome: a shared understanding of why the project exists and what success looks like.
  • NOT list. Data: deferred features, future-phase ideas, non-goals. Outcome: scope creep prevented and false consensus eliminated.
  • Trade-off sliders. Data: fixed-vs-flexible positions for scope, cost, time, quality and partners. Outcome: agreed rules of engagement for future trade-off decisions.

The pattern to remember: every activity has a deliverable. Nothing is a discussion that ends in nothing.

15. Which artifact does each inception activity produce?
  • Stakeholder identification: stakeholder map
  • Persona creation: personas (plus empathy map)
  • Current-state mapping: as-is journey map - steps, touchpoints, pain points
  • Problem definition: problem statement / "How might we" questions
  • Visioning and goal setting: product vision, goals and success metrics (OKRs)
  • Scope boundaries: in / out of scope, the NOT list
  • Future-state design: to-be journey map and service blueprint
  • Feature discovery: user story map
  • Release slicing: MVP definition and release plan / roadmap
  • Epic definition: epics, cut from the map's release slices
  • Risk identification: RAID log (risks, assumptions, issues, dependencies)
16. Which skill produces which artifact, and what does each one read?
  • discovery-users: stakeholder map, personas. Reads the idea and research material
  • as-is-journey: as-is journey, problem statement. Reads personas.md
  • product-vision: vision, goals and metrics, in / out of scope. Reads the problem statement and personas
  • to-be-journey: to-be journey, service blueprint. Reads the as-is journey and vision
  • story-map: user story map, MVP and release plan. Reads the to-be journey and blueprint
  • epics: epics, one release slice per run. Reads the story map and release plan
  • user-stories: user stories, one epic per run. Reads the epic and story map

Each skill reads the files of the step before it and writes its own, so the chain stays traceable.

17. What is the difference between an output and an outcome?

An output is a thing you shipped. An outcome is a measurable change in user or business behaviour.

"The system is deployed" is an output. "Average handling time per case drops from 25 days to 5" is an outcome. Inception agrees the outcomes, because outputs are not success.

18. What belongs on a NOT list, and why is it one of the highest-value hours of the week?

Everything deliberately not delivered in this horizon: deferred features, future-phase ideas and explicit non-goals.

It is high value because it kills false consensus. Stakeholders who all nodded at the vision often had different products in their heads; the NOT list is where that surfaces - in a workshop, rather than in sprint 9.

19. What is an MVP, and what is a learning goal?

An MVP is the smallest thing you build in order to learn whether it is worth continuing. It is not "version one with fewer features" and not a prototype.

So every MVP carries learning goals - the assumptions it is designed to test, written as questions. If you cannot say what you would learn, you have a release, not an MVP.

20. Where does slicing happen?

At two levels - epic and story. It is not a single step.

  • Epic level. The release slices are drawn on the story map: each horizontal line cuts through every journey step and becomes an MVP or a release. Epics are then cut from those slices, which is what makes them vertical by design.
  • Story level. Stories are split again inside each epic, using patterns such as SPIDR (spike, path, interface, data, rules) - or by workflow step, data type, business rule, or happy path first.

The same rule applies at both levels: never slice by technical layer - "database", "API", "UI". Every epic and every story must deliver something a user can see working.

21. How do you decide what goes in the first release?

Sequence by pain and by risk, not by what is easiest to build. Take the top-ranked pain point theme first, and prefer slices that need the least unproven technology, so the team learns fast at low risk.

Prioritisation techniques: MoSCoW (must, should, could, won't), or a business-value vs technical-risk matrix. Work that depends on an unproven capability belongs behind a spike, not in the MVP.

22. Name the traceability ID chain carried through the inception artifacts.

Stakeholder → persona → pain point → as-is step → goal / capability → to-be step → story-map card → epic → user story.

Typically SH-, P-, PP-, A-, G- / C-, T-, S-, E-, US-. Carrying the IDs is what lets an auditor walk back from a line of code to the pain point it serves.

23. What is a proto-persona, and what is the risk of using one?

A persona built from the team's own knowledge - a process document and a workshop - rather than from user research or interviews.

It is a legitimate starting point when there is no research yet, but it must be labelled as such. The risk is assumptions hardening into fact: nobody remembers which parts were evidence, so "what users feel" stays unknown while being quoted as if it were known. Mark every unvalidated figure and validate it early.

24. What is the Product Box for?

A creative exercise where the team designs the product's physical box or a marketing headline: the top three features, a slogan and the key hooks.

It forces the team to say what the product is actually for, in the customer's words. If three people design three different boxes, the vision was not as shared as everyone thought.

25. How does a risk identified at inception become work?

Each risk is named, given a severity and probability, and paired with a mitigation - and that mitigation becomes an epic or a story with an owner.

That is how inception feeds the backlog: the epic map is not invented, it is derived from scope plus risks. A risk with no epic is a risk nobody owns.

26. What is an epic map and what is it used for?

A visual map of all epics, arranged by capability (left to right) and priority or release (top to bottom). It shows the whole product at a glance and is used to slice releases and milestones horizontally.

27. Which estimation technique is used for epics and which for stories?
  • Epics: T-shirt sizing (XS, S, M, L, XL)
  • Stories: story points with Planning Poker (1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13)

An XL epic is a signal to split it. Story points measure complexity, effort and uncertainty, relative to a reference story.

28. What do you do with an epic you cannot estimate confidently?

Record the low confidence rather than hiding it behind a number, and ask what the estimate depends on - usually an unproven capability or data that does not exist yet.

Then plan a spike early to retire that uncertainty, flag the epic as a risk, and give a confidence range instead of a single figure. An estimate nobody believes is worse than an admitted unknown.

4. Epics

29. What is an epic, and where does it sit in the hierarchy?

A large body of work that delivers one clear business outcome. It is too big for one sprint and is broken into stories.

Initiative / Theme, Epic, Story, Task.

30. Why do epics matter?

Alignment, prioritisation, roadmap and funding, traceability (idea to epic to story to commit to test), and scope control.

31. Name the sections of a good epic.

Title, problem / context, outcome and success metrics, users / personas, scope and out of scope, epic acceptance criteria, NFRs, dependencies and risks, size, milestone.

32. How do you tell an epic from a story, and what size should an epic be?

An epic delivers a whole capability and spans several sprints; a story is one slice of it that fits inside a single sprint. If it cannot be finished in one sprint, it is an epic.

Sized in T-shirts: anything reaching XL is a signal to split. A useful cross-check is that the epic's size should roughly match the sum of its stories' points - if an epic is sized M but its stories total 60 points, one of the two estimates is wrong.

33. Why does every epic need an explicit out-of-scope section?

Because neighbouring epics overlap, and without a boundary two teams either build the same thing twice or both assume the other is doing it.

Naming what the epic does not cover - and which epic covers it instead - prevents scope creep, makes dependencies visible, and keeps the epic small enough to finish. It is the NOT list applied one level down.

5. Stories and INVEST

34. What is the user story format, and what are the 3 Cs?

As a persona I want capability so that benefit. The "so that" is the most important part.

The 3 Cs: Card (short description), Conversation (discussion), Confirmation (acceptance criteria).

35. What does INVEST stand for?
  • Independent
  • Negotiable
  • Valuable
  • Estimable
  • Small
  • Testable

If a story fails Estimable, run a spike. Fails Small, split it. Fails Testable, the acceptance criteria are missing.

36. What is the Given / When / Then format used for?

Acceptance criteria. They cover the happy path, edge cases and errors, and become test cases directly.

37. What must a story's acceptance criteria cover before it is Ready?

Not just the happy path. A complete set covers:

  • The happy path - the expected input produces the expected result
  • Edge cases - empty, partial, duplicate or boundary input
  • Errors - what the user sees when a dependency fails
  • The negative case - what must not happen, such as nothing being sent or saved until a person approves

Write them as Given / When / Then so each one becomes a test case directly. The negative case is the one teams forget, and it is usually the one that matters for safety.

38. How do you cross-check an epic's size against its stories?

Add up the stories' points and divide by the team's velocity: that gives the number of sprints, which should match the epic's T-shirt size.

For example, an epic broken into six stories totalling 34 points, at a velocity of about 20 per sprint, is roughly two sprints - consistent with an L. If the two numbers disagree, either the epic is mis-sized or stories are missing. Doing this early is cheaper than discovering it in sprint planning.

39. At what size should a story be split, and name three splitting patterns.

At 13 points or more. Patterns: by workflow step, by data or type, by business rule, by interface, happy path first, spike.

40. What is the difference between the Definition of Ready and the Definition of Done?

DoR protects developers from vague stories (story format, G/W/T criteria, NFRs, estimated, dependencies known, small enough). DoD protects users from half-done work (reviewed PR merged, criteria pass, tests green, no critical findings, gates pass, docs updated, PO accepted).

6. Dev cycle: Plan, Code, Review

41. What is the gate at each step of Plan, Code, Review and Test?
  • Plan: tech lead approves the plan before code
  • Code: developer steers and checks each phase
  • Review: reviewer approves the PR
  • Test: QA confirms the tests prove the acceptance criteria
42. Why must tests come from the acceptance criteria and not from the code?

Otherwise the AI "tests its own homework": it generates tests that just confirm whatever the code does.

43. Which AI skill is used at each step from idea to review?
  • Idea to Vision: idea-to-vision
  • Vision to Epic: epic-creation
  • Epic to Stories: story-mode
  • Story to Plan: plan-mode
  • Plan to Code: code-generation
  • Code to Review: review-mode

7. Sprints and milestones

44. Name the five sprint ceremonies and their purpose.
  • Sprint Planning: pick stories, commit to a sprint goal
  • Daily Stand-up: sync, surface blockers
  • Backlog Refinement: prepare upcoming stories (DoR)
  • Sprint Review: demo working software, get feedback
  • Retrospective: improve the process
45. What is velocity, and what should you never do with it?

Story points completed per sprint (average of the last 3). It is a planning tool for the team. Never use it as a performance metric or compare it across teams.

46. What is a milestone, and what must every milestone have?

A business checkpoint - not a date on its own. Every milestone needs a goal, a scope of users, and exit criteria you can objectively test.

A common progression is MVP (prove the value, often on replayed or historic data), then Pilot (real users, but a limited group), then GA (everyone, fully supported and auditable). If you cannot state how you would know a milestone is finished, it is a wish, not a milestone.

47. What determines the order of epics across milestones?
  • Dependency: anything other epics need to finish must come first.
  • Risk: the riskiest assumption is tested as early as possible, so a failure is cheap.
  • Exposure: any control that protects a real user - review, approval, permissions, privacy - must land before the milestone that first puts real users in front of the system.

That third one catches teams out. A safeguard scheduled after the pilot is a safeguard that arrived too late.

48. Why do platform and enabler epics cluster in the MVP?

Because on a self-hosted or greenfield stack the shared foundations - connectors and integrations, identity, data access, the review and approval path - have to exist before any user-facing epic can be finished.

The trap is that a milestone full of platform work delivers no visible value. So pair each enabler with the first feature that proves it, and keep the MVP honest: it must still demonstrate something to a user.

49. What does traceability give you?

Every artifact links to the one before it: Idea → Vision → Epic → Story → Plan → Commit / PR → Tests → Done.

It means anyone - an auditor, a new joiner, the team in six months - can walk backwards from a line of code to the user problem that justified it, and forwards from a problem to the evidence it was solved. In regulated or audited contexts it is not just good practice, it is a product requirement: every decision must be traceable to a named approver.

Wrap-up

50. What stays with people, even when AI accelerates every step?

Accountability. A human gate approves at every step.

51. Retro: where did AI help most, where did it get it wrong, and who caught it?

Open question for your team. Typically AI finds consistency issues, while humans find "this isn't what the PO meant".
